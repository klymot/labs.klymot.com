Is "Average" Really the Average?

Every daily temperature in the old weather books is just the day's highest and lowest, added together and halved. This lab lets you check, for a real weather station, whether that matches averaging the temperature all day long — and what you could do about it if it doesn't.

Pick a station. Measure the gap between the two kinds of average yourself, day by day and month by month, see whether it has a shape, then test whether a fix built on part of the record still works on years you kept back.

  • What a daily "average" temperature even is — and that there is more than one way to work it out.
  • How to measure the gap between the two, for a station you choose.

Why did anyone ever use just the highest and lowest?

For most of the history of weather-keeping, the thermometer you could afford recorded only two things about a day: the highest it got and the lowest it fell to. A special pair of thermometers held onto those two marks until someone came round once a day to read and reset them. Nobody was standing there taking a reading every hour. So the day's "average" became the one thing those instruments made easy: the high and the low, added together and halved. Billions of daily records — the backbone of the temperature history — were built that way.

What do we mean by the "true" all-day average?

On this page the all-day average means adding up the readings taken through the day and dividing by how many there were — so every part of the day gets a say, not just the two extremes. It is still only a sampling of a smooth curve (readings every hour, or every few minutes, rather than truly continuous), so it is not perfect either — but it uses far more of the day than two numbers do.

Why might the seasons differ?

Here is one idea to hold in mind — not the answer. A day's shape depends on things that change with the season: how long the sun is up, how quickly the air warms in the morning versus how slowly it cools at night. If the shape of the day changes through the year, the gap between the two averages might change with it too. Whether that is what is going on at your station — and which way it goes — is for the chart to show, not this box to tell.

Why test on hidden years?

If you check a fix using the same data you built it from, you are marking your own homework — of course it looks good, you tuned it to those years. The honest way is to build the fix on some years, then try it on other years it has never seen. Whatever leftover error shows up on those hidden years is a fairer guess at how the fix would do on records you can't check.

What is the "leftover"?

Even the best calendar-based fix can't know what the weather did on a particular day — a stormy week, a still clear night — and that day-to-day wobble is what's left after any correction. It's the part no fix built from the calendar can remove. It gets smaller when you average more days or more years together, because the wobbles partly cancel out.

So — is "average" really the average?

You've now seen one station's answer. It may not be the next station's — the size, the direction, the seasonal shape and the year-to-year wander can all be different somewhere else. The only way to know is to look.

What is this test?

It is a one-way ANOVA — a standard way to ask whether groups really differ, or whether the differences you see could just be the ordinary wobble inside each group. Here the groups are your stations. It compares how much the average gap changes between stations against how much the gap bounces around within a single station, and reports that as a ratio called F. An F near 1 would mean the stations look alike — one fix might serve them all. A large F means each station's fix is really its own, so a correction worked out in one place cannot simply be carried to another. The more stations you add, the more you have to go on.

A puzzle we can't settle (for the curious)

At one of the long stations, the year-to-year gap doesn't just wander — it swings up and down slowly, over decades, in a way that happens to line up with a known back-and-forth cycle in North Atlantic ocean temperatures (called the AMO). Intriguing — but two very different stories could explain it, and this lab can't tell them apart:

  • It could be a real ocean-and-climate signal, showing up in the day's true extremes in a way the old thermometers caught.
  • Or it could be slow changes in the instruments and how the station was run over the decades, which happen to follow a similar curve.

The record is also too short to be sure the swing is even a repeating cycle rather than a single long wander. It's left here unresolved, on purpose — a real open question, not a tidy ending.

What the lab computes
For each station, the gap is the daily midpoint minus the all-day average. The lab aggregates that gap by calendar month (the seasonal shape), by year (the long view), and runs the correction test by fitting each strategy on one block of years and measuring its leftover error on a held-back block — never scoring a fix on the years it was built from.

This is a neutral exploration tool. No way of averaging is endorsed as the true one, and no correction strategy is endorsed as the right one.
