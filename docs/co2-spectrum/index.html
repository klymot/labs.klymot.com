What Does More CO₂ Actually Change?

Every argument about CO₂ eventually lands on one picture — its absorption spectrum — and then splits.

Every one of them is pointing at something real on the same curve. Almost none of them has looked at it.

This lab computes that curve in front of you — from the measured molecular data the professionals use, through a real column of air that weather balloons actually measured — and hands you the controls. By the end you will know what each of them is shouting about, and be able to decide for yourself what is actually going on. Nobody here tells you the answer.

A few words need explaining first: what a spectrum is, and what "saturated" really means. If you already know, skip ahead — the experiment starts at choose what to compare against.

What is a spectrum?

White light is a mix of colours. A prism, or a raindrop, sorts them: each colour is light of a different frequency, and each frequency bends by a slightly different amount on the way through. This is why raindrops make a rainbow. Laid out in order, the colours form a spectrum.

The spectrum carries on past what our eyes can see. Beyond violet the light keeps bending more: ultraviolet, then X-rays. Beyond red it bends less: infrared, then microwaves and radio. Infrared is the one you can feel rather than see — the warmth of a fire on your face, and the heat the ground gives off at night. Everything on this page happens there.

When chemists and physicists say "spectrum" they mean one of two kinds of graph, and both have the colours along the bottom. Each colour can be named by three numbers, and every chart on this page shows all three: its wavelength, how long one wave is, in micrometres (μm); its frequency, how many waves pass per second, in terahertz (THz, a million million per second); and the spectroscopist's favourite, its wavenumber, how many waves fit in one centimetre (cm⁻¹). Frequency and wavenumber rise toward the blue; wavelength falls. The charts are laid out by wavenumber, low on the left to high on the right, which is why the infrared's long, slow waves sit on the left. The first kind of graph is an emission spectrum: how much light a source gives off at each colour. The sun's peaks just short of the visible, in the near infrared, and spills across it; the warm ground's peaks deep in the infrared.

Here are the two emission spectra that matter on this page: the sun's, and the ground's.

Wait — in the global-average view the ground gives off more than the sun sends. Is the Earth losing energy?

No; the two totals are not the same kind of quantity, so they need not match. The 340 W/m² is what arrives from the sun, spread over the whole globe. The 391 W/m² is what a 15 °C surface radiates — and the ground is 15 °C because it is warmed by more than the sun: the air above it radiates too, and some of that comes down. What must balance is different: sunlight in against everything the whole planet, ground and air together, sends out to space from the top of the atmosphere. That is a different accounting, and this chart is not it — it shows what each of two glowing bodies emits, nothing about where it ends up.

Absorption

Put something in the light's way and not all of it comes out the other side. A gas takes energy from the light only at the colours its molecules can accept — for CO₂ and water vapour, particular colours in the infrared — and lets the rest straight through. Measure what comes out, compare it with what went in, and you have the second kind of graph: an absorption spectrum, showing at each colour how much was taken out. Every chart on this page from here on is one of those.

Absorption has a limit that turns out to matter enormously: once all the light at a frequency has been absorbed, there is none left to absorb. More of the gas can't take more than everything. The curve stops — physicists call it saturation; think of it as clipping.

Labs measure absorption on two scales, and the chart below can show either. Transmittance is the fraction of the light that gets through: 1 means all of it, 0 means none. Absorbance counts the same thing in factors of ten: absorbance 1 means a tenth gets through, 2 a hundredth, 3 a thousandth. Absorbance is the chemist's favourite because it grows in a straight line with the amount of gas — double the gas, double the absorbance — right up until the detector runs out of light, somewhere past absorbance 3, and can no longer tell 3 from 30. Transmittance is what a detector actually sees, and it shows the same limit as a floor at zero.

Switch on CO₂ 430 ppm, about the air outside today. On the absorbance scale it is the trace curve simply stretched upward — same shape, forty times taller — which is exactly the problem: the tops of the strong bands run into the ceiling where the detector reads nothing, and are flattened against it. That is clipping. Out in the wings of each band, where the absorbance is still small, the curve still rises with every extra bit of gas. Flip to transmittance and the same thing shows as band centres pinned to the floor at zero.

This is why the argument at the top of the page never resolves. One side points at the middle of a band: it's clipped, so more CO₂ changes nothing there. The other points at the edges: they aren't clipped, so more CO₂ keeps widening the clipped part. Both are looking at the same curve, and each is right about the place it points at. Neither tells you how much, between which two amounts, through what air — and that is what the rest of this page lets you work out for yourself.

CO₂ is not the only gas in the air that absorbs infrared; water vapour does too, and the second argument from the top of the page is about the two of them: does CO₂'s absorption sit under water vapour's own, or in the open? Switch on water vapour in the legend above — a trace, and then a typical ground-level humidity — and look at where the two overlap and where each has the spectrum to itself. One caution goes with the green curves: humidity is the one thing no textbook can tell you. It varies enormously from place to place and day to day, and it has to be measured — which is why, further down, you will pick a real place.

The arithmetic of clipping

Absorption compounds like layers of tinted glass, not like a dimmer switch. Each wavelength has its own total "optical depth" — how much absorbing power the sample stacks up — and the fraction passed is an exponential of it: e−depth. Absorbance is that depth on a base-10 scale, which is why it grows in a straight line with the amount of gas (the Beer–Lambert law) while the light getting through collapses: e−0.02 versus e−0.01 is barely a change, e−2 versus e−1 is a large one, and e−200 versus e−100 are both indistinguishable from zero. Which parts of the spectrum are in which regime, at the amounts being compared, is exactly what the charts show.

What is HITRAN?

HITRAN is the standard public database of molecular absorption lines, maintained at the Harvard–Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics and built from decades of laboratory measurements. For each of hundreds of thousands of lines it records the position, strength, and shape parameters. It is what atmospheric scientists, astronomers, and instrument builders all compute from. The spectra here were computed line by line from HITRAN's CO₂ and water-vapour data — every line between 400 and 8000 cm⁻¹ above a small strength threshold.

What is the "standard atmosphere"?

The lab sample above is not a real cell in a real lab: it is the bottom 750 m slab of the US Standard Atmosphere 1976 — one temperature (15 °C), one pressure (sea level) — rescaled to a 100 m path, with the absorption lines computed from HITRAN at those conditions. The US Standard Atmosphere is a reference description of how temperature and pressure change with height, a single idealised column used across aviation and atmospheric science as a common yardstick; it describes no particular place and says nothing about humidity. The "typical humidity" curve uses the companion "U.S. Standard" water-vapour profile from the Air Force Geophysics Laboratory's 1986 reference atmospheres — about 0.8 % water by volume at ground level. Fair for the shapes of the two spectra; the wrong tool for anything that depends on how humid a real place is, which is why, further down, you will pick one that was actually measured.

What exactly is computed — and what isn't?

Computed: the transmittance of the whole vertical column — the fraction of light at each wavelength that would make it through the atmosphere from bottom to top — and, later on the page, the altitude at which light heading out to space at each wavelength typically had its last interaction. Both come from adding up each gas's absorption, layer by layer, at the temperature and pressure of each layer.

Not computed: anything about what the atmosphere emits, how energy balances out, or what any of it implies for temperatures. Those are different calculations with more moving parts, and this lab doesn't do them or take a position on them. It shows you the absorption — the part the two arguments above are actually about — and stops there.

And everything shown is conditional on this model's ingredients: the atmosphere in use, HITRAN's line data, and the idealisations listed under Data sources & methodology at the bottom of the page. What you discover here is what this construction shows — that's true of every calculation of this kind, stated or not.

Pick a real place

The chart at the top ran through a textbook atmosphere with a textbook humidity. To compute anything real — how much water vapour actually sits over CO₂'s bands, how much energy the air actually takes, how high it happens — you need a real column of air, and above all real humidity, because that is the ingredient no textbook can supply.

So pick a real place. Each of these is a weather-balloon station whose balloons recorded temperature, pressure, and humidity from the ground to about 30 km, thousands of times over five years. From here on, every chart is computed through the atmosphere that station actually measured, water vapour included — starting with the live spectrum that opens below once you choose.

How much radiation does the air absorb?

But wait — there's more. So far you have been comparing one column of air at one amount against the same column at another, wavelength by wavelength. Now the sun and the ground get their turn.

Each spectrum now collapses to a single number, and there are two to ask for. Of the sunlight coming down, what share does the column absorb? Of the heat the ground sends up, what share? The chart below gives either total — switch direction at the top of it — and, under the total, who took what: CO₂'s take and water vapour's take, following the light in its direction of travel.

That split is where people talk past each other. One person points at a gas's take and says it grew; another points at the total and says it hardly moved; a third points at the other gas's take and says it shrank. Any of them may be describing the same chart — if the light one gas took would otherwise have been taken by the other further along the beam, the total moves less than either take, which is robbing Peter to pay Paul. Or the total may move with the take. Watch the total and the two takes together, in each direction, and every one of those claims has somewhere to land.

This is an absorption calculation, not a climate model. Held fixed while you move the CO₂: the station's measured temperature, pressure and humidity, level by level; a vertical beam; CO₂ mixed evenly through the column; no clouds, scattering, or other gases; and no re-emission by the air of what it absorbs. The numbers below are the share of each source's energy the column absorbs on the way through — nothing more is claimed for them.

What exactly does this chart count, in each direction?

Incoming sunlight: the sun is modelled as an ideal blackbody at its standard effective temperature (5772 K), and the shares are of its total energy across all wavelengths. Absorption is computed for CO₂ and water vapour only, over the wavelengths where those two gases absorb (out to 0.83 μm for CO₂ and 0.56 μm for H₂O); sunlight outside those ranges — including all visible and ultraviolet light — is treated as passing untouched. The real atmosphere also scatters sunlight and absorbs some through other gases (ozone, oxygen) and clouds; none of that is in this number. Every point is weighted by how much solar energy actually arrives at each wavelength — the same top-of-atmosphere solar spectrum you can overlay on the spectrum chart — so these are shares of energy, not of wavelengths. The split into CO₂'s take and water vapour's take follows the beam layer by layer in its direction of travel, dividing each layer's absorption between the gases in proportion to their optical depths there; it is computed in your browser over the wavelengths shipped to it (1.25–25 μm), and CO₂'s weak remaining bands at shorter wavelengths, from the offline calculation, are credited to CO₂ in full, with water vapour taking the remainder of the total. For scale, 1 % of the sun's energy is about 14 W for every square metre facing an overhead sun (the top-of-atmosphere flux is about 1361 W/m²).

Who took what: the split into CO₂'s take and water vapour's take follows each beam layer by layer in its direction of travel — sunlight meets the dry upper air first, the ground's heat meets the moist lower air first — dividing each layer's absorption between the gases in proportion to their optical depths there. Water's take is therefore not fixed: it is what is left for water after CO₂ has had its turn along the beam, so it moves when only the CO₂ changes. A faint comparison line, off by default, shows what CO₂ would take in dry air.

Outgoing heat: the ground is modelled as an ideal blackbody at this station's own median surface temperature from the same 2015–2019 soundings (also drawable on the spectrum chart), and the share counted is the fraction of that emitted energy the column absorbs somewhere on the way up — the part that does not reach space directly. Here the computed wavelengths cover about 99 % of the ground's emission, including water vapour's far-infrared rotational band down to 100 cm⁻¹; CO₂ genuinely has no absorption below 400 cm⁻¹, so nothing of its effect is cut off. One thing this deliberately does not include: the atmosphere re-emits what it absorbs, in all directions — following that energy is a different calculation, and this lab stops at the absorption.

Why does water vapour's line move when only the CO₂ changes?

The amount of water vapour in your column never changes — it is what the balloons measured. What changes is how much light is left for it. Where both gases absorb at the same wavelength, whichever the beam meets first takes from the undiminished beam and the other gets what remains; sunlight meets CO₂ in the dry upper air before it reaches the moist lower air, while the ground's heat meets the water first. So as CO₂ rises, water vapour's take can fall — the light it would have absorbed has already gone — and the total the column absorbs is always the sum of the two takes. That total, by the way, does not depend on the order: absorption multiplies, and a product does not care which factor came first.

How high does the absorption reach?

So far every chart has treated the whole column at once. But absorption happens somewhere. At a colour where the air is nearly transparent, light from the ground travels all the way out to space; at a colour where it absorbs strongly, that light is gone within the first few hundred metres and never gets anywhere near the top. The chart below asks where, in two mirror-image ways — switch between them at the top of it.

Climbing up from the ground. Imagine rising through the air with an instrument tuned to one colour, looking down. At some height the ground has faded to the point where it is effectively out of sight — the light from it has been cut to a fraction 1/e, optical depth 1. The climbing up view plots that height at every colour. Where the ground never fades out, the curve sits at the top of our atmosphere, 70 km: the air is transparent there and you would see the ground from space.

Coming down from the top. Now start at the top of the atmosphere with the same instrument, looking up, and descend. At some depth the top has faded out of sight. The coming down view plots that height. Where the top never fades, the curve sits on the ground: the air is transparent there and you would see space from the surface.

Both views draw your reference in gold and the slider's amount in blue. Where the two curves lie on top of each other, changing the CO₂ moved that height nowhere; where they part, it moved it — and by how much is read straight off the axis.

What exactly does "out of sight" mean here?

Optical depth 1: the point along the path at which the light from where you started has been attenuated to 1/e, about 37 %. It is the standard yardstick for "where the absorption effectively happens" at a given colour — beyond it, most of what you would see is the air itself rather than what lies behind it. The two charts count that depth from opposite ends of the same column, so at a strongly absorbing colour the first sits low and the second sits high, and at a transparent colour each sits at the far end. Nothing new is computed for these charts: they re-read the same layer-by-layer absorption that produced every chart above.

"Out of sight" — but where does the energy go?

A gas absorbs and emits at the same colours: the absorption spectrum you have been looking at is also CO₂'s emission spectrum (this is Kirchhoff's law). So the air that takes in the ground's light at a colour also gives off light at that colour, at its own temperature — in every direction. What space sees at that colour is therefore not the ground but the air at the height where the column first comes into view, and the temperature there sets how bright it looks.

This lab stops at the heights. Following the energy from there — how much comes back down, how the temperature at each height responds — is a different calculation with more moving parts, and no number on this page claims to be that.

The exact recipe behind these two charts

For a technically minded reader, here is the calculation with nothing left implicit:

  • Criterion: cumulative optical depth τ = 1, integrated vertically — layer by layer upward from the surface for the first chart, downward from 70 km for the second. CO₂ absorption only, at the concentration each curve is drawn for.
  • Atmosphere: your chosen station's median measured temperature and pressure profile (2015–2019 soundings), extended above balloon top with a standard atmosphere — the shaded region. Each layer's absorption is computed at that layer's own temperature and pressure, so pressure broadening genuinely narrows with altitude.
  • Mixing ratios: CO₂ is treated as uniformly mixed through the whole column at the slider's value — a good approximation for a well-mixed gas, and an assumption, stated here. Water vapour (where it appears elsewhere on this page) is never assumed mixed: it follows the measured humidity level by level.
  • Spectral resolution: the underlying absorption is computed line by line at 0.01 cm⁻¹ (finer at high altitude), then each 2 cm⁻¹ display bin keeps an 8-point distribution of its absorption strengths rather than one average — this is what lets saturated line cores stay saturated. The plotted height is the weighted mean of the eight crossing heights within each bin; monochromatic heights at single wavelengths inside a bin can sit above or below it.
  • Where τ = 1 is never reached: the ground never drops out of sight, so the climbing-up view places the point at the top of our atmosphere, 70 km; the top never drops out of sight, so the coming-down view places it on the ground. In both views a bin counts as "never" when less than half its quadrature weight crosses; the plotted height is the weighted mean over the points that do.

The remaining ingredients — line data, wing cutoffs, the quadrature — are under Data sources & methodology at the bottom of the page.

Where does the extra absorption happen?

Here is a place where two people can look at the same column and talk past each other. Sunlight comes down through the dry upper air first and reaches the moist lower air last; the ground's heat goes up through the moist air first. The column total is the same whichever way the light travels — absorption multiplies, and order doesn't change a product. But where the extra absorption from more CO₂ lands, and how much of it is light that water vapour would otherwise have absorbed, is different for the two directions. This chart computes it layer by layer, between your reference and the slider: blue is the change in what CO₂ absorbs in each layer, green the change in what water vapour absorbs there, each as a share of the source's total energy per kilometre of altitude. Negative means that gas absorbs less than it did at your reference.

How this chart is computed, and what it leaves out

For each wavelength bin and each of its eight absorption-strength points, a beam is followed through the column in its direction of travel — top layer first for sunlight, bottom layer first for the ground's heat. In each layer the fraction of the remaining beam that is absorbed is split between CO₂ and water vapour in proportion to their optical depths in that layer. The results are weighted by how much of the source's energy arrives at each wavelength (an ideal 5772 K sun; the ground at this station's own median surface temperature) and summed, once at your reference and once at the slider's value; the chart shows the difference, divided by each layer's thickness. It covers the wavelengths this page ships to your browser, 1.25 to 25 μm — the readout says what share of each source's energy that is. CO₂'s remaining bands at shorter wavelengths are weak, so the change from CO₂ is nearly complete. The same exclusions apply as everywhere on this page (see Data sources).

So — what does more CO₂ actually change?

You've now seen one atmosphere's answer, at concentrations you chose yourself: which parts of the spectrum moved between your reference and your comparison, where water vapour shares the window, how the absorbed shares of incoming sunlight and outgoing ground heat ran across the sweep you made, and where the two τ=1 heights shifted or stood still. A different place — or a different pair of values — may paint a different picture. The only way to know is to look.

What the lab computes
Voigt line shapes with air broadening, a 25 cm⁻¹ line-wing cutoff and a 10⁻²⁷ cm/molecule intensity threshold, computed per atmospheric layer at the profile's temperature and pressure, then reduced to an 8-point correlated-k distribution per 2 cm⁻¹ display bin (the standard reduction used in atmospheric band models; it preserves how line cores saturate at any concentration). CO₂ is treated as uniformly mixed through the column at the slider's concentration; its optical depth is stored per unit concentration and scaled by your slider — exact to better than 0.2% up to 5000 ppm, since CO₂'s self-broadening is negligible at these amounts. H₂O is never assumed mixed: it is computed once at the measured level-by-level humidity with proper self-broadening. The combined view multiplies the two transmittances (random-overlap assumption). H₂O includes the MT_CKD 4.3 continuum (self and foreign, per layer), with the 25 cm⁻¹ line pedestal subtracted first as that model assumes. Not included: CO₂ line mixing (a small reshaping of the densest band cores). The coming-down height is the g-weighted altitude where cumulative optical depth from the top of the atmosphere reaches 1; the climbing-up height mirrors it from the surface upward. In both, a bin where less than half the quadrature weight ever reaches optical depth 1 is drawn at the far end of the column — the ground or 70 km — as "never". The absorbed-energy curves integrate the same computed transmittances against ideal blackbody spectra — the sun at 5772 K (with the line data extended offline to 12000 cm⁻¹ for CO₂ and 18000 cm⁻¹ for H₂O), and the ground at each station's own median surface temperature (with H₂O's far-infrared rotational band computed down to 100 cm⁻¹; CO₂ has no absorption below 400 cm⁻¹). Shares are of each source's total energy, for a vertical beam, with radiation outside the computed ranges treated as untouched; scattering, clouds, other gases, and the atmosphere's own re-emission are excluded throughout.

This is a neutral exploration tool. No concentration is endorsed as a baseline, and no reading of the spectrum is endorsed as the important one.
